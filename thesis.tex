    \chapter{Introduction}

Clinical information is progressively collected and stored in structured
digital form. Many clinical prediction problems are naturally represented as
tabular data composed of demographic characteristics, physiological
measurements, laboratory values, medical history, and other patient-related
variables. Such data support a wide range of predictive tasks, including
disease identification, risk estimation, and clinical outcome prediction
\cite{rajkomar2019mlmedicine}. However, medical tabular data also present
several challenges for predictive modeling. Compared with the large-scale
datasets commonly available in computer vision and natural language processing,
medical datasets are often relatively small, may contain heterogeneous feature
types, and can exhibit substantial differences in class distribution and
predictive structure across clinical tasks.

Traditional machine learning methods, particularly tree-based models, remain
strong approaches for tabular prediction. Gradient-boosted decision trees such
as XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost demonstrated competitive performance
across a wide range of structured-data problems
\cite{chen2016xgboost,ke2017lightgbm,prokhorenkova2018catboost}. In contrast,
the advantages of deep learning on conventional tabular datasets have been less
consistent than in image, text, and speech applications. Previous studies
showed that carefully tuned tree-based models frequently remain competitive with
or outperform deep neural networks on typical tabular benchmarks
\cite{grinsztajn2022tree}. Nevertheless, recent advances in tabular deep
learning introduced architectures designed specifically to improve the
representation of numerical and categorical variables and to model interactions
between features and observations
\cite{gorishniy2021revisiting,gorishniy2022embeddings,somepalli2021saint}.

One important direction within this development is retrieval-augmented tabular
learning. Rather than generating a prediction solely from parameters learned
during training, retrieval-augmented models additionally use information from
relevant observations stored in the training set. For a given target sample,
similar observations are retrieved and incorporated into the prediction
process. This idea connects representation learning with local information from
the available data and has motivated several retrieval-based approaches for
tabular prediction
\cite{qin2021rim,kossen2021selfattention,gorishniy2024tabr}.

TabR is a recent retrieval-augmented deep learning model for tabular data that
combines a feed-forward architecture with a nearest-neighbor-like retrieval
mechanism operating in a learned representation space \cite{gorishniy2024tabr}.
TabR demonstrated that an appropriately designed retrieval mechanism can
substantially improve the competitiveness of deep learning for tabular
prediction, but the original study also showed that retrieval is not uniformly
beneficial for every dataset.

The behavior of a retrieval-augmented model depends not only on how candidate
observations are retrieved, but also on the representation from which
similarity is determined. In TabR, numerical and categorical inputs are encoded
into learned sample representations before candidate retrieval is performed.
The retrieval component therefore operates on representations whose geometry
depends on how the original input variables are transformed. However, the
original TabR architecture does not explicitly introduce sample-specific
reweighting of individual numerical variables before the encoder. Its
specialized attention-like mechanism is instead primarily associated with the
retrieval and aggregation of candidate observations.

This distinction motivates the central question investigated in this thesis.
Medical tabular datasets commonly contain numerical measurements whose
predictive relevance may vary across prediction tasks and individual samples.
An explicit mechanism that allows the numerical representation to adapt to the
characteristics of each observation may therefore influence the representation
subsequently used by the retrieval model. At the same time, modern neural
networks are already capable of learning nonlinear transformations of their
inputs, and it cannot be assumed in advance that an additional feature-aware
mechanism will consistently improve predictive performance. The effect of such
a modification must therefore be evaluated empirically and separated from
changes to the retrieval architecture itself.

To investigate this problem, this thesis proposes FiTabR, a feature-aware
extension of TabR for medical tabular classification. FiTabR introduces a
lightweight attention network that operates on numerical features before the
TabR encoder. For each input sample, the attention module produces a
distribution over the numerical variables. These values are then used to
modulate the numerical input through residual feature reweighting, which
preserves the original feature values and only increases the emphasis on
selected features. The resulting representation is subsequently processed by
the original TabR encoder and retrieval pipeline.

This controlled design makes it possible to examine whether changes in
pre-retrieval numerical representation can affect the behavior of a
retrieval-augmented model without simultaneously redesigning its retrieval
mechanism. Accordingly, the primary research question of this thesis is:

\begin{quote}
Can sample-specific numerical feature modulation provide useful improvements
to retrieval-augmented tabular classification while preserving the underlying
TabR retrieval framework?
\end{quote}
Two additional questions follow from this objective. First, if differences in
performance are observed, which components of the proposed representation
mechanism are responsible for them? Second, does the learned feature-attention
mechanism provide meaningful information about how the model emphasizes
numerical clinical variables across different observations?

To address these questions, FiTabR was evaluated on three medical binary
classification tasks: Heart Disease, Pima Diabetes, and Stroke Prediction.
These datasets provide different experimental conditions in terms of sample
size, feature composition, and class distribution. FiTabR was compared directly
with the original TabR architecture and with conventional machine learning and
deep tabular learning baselines.

The experimental findings provide partial rather than universal support for
the initial hypothesis. FiTabR showed its clearest improvement relative to TabR
on the Heart Disease dataset, where accuracy increased from 0.8841 to 0.8937
and cross-entropy decreased from 0.3586 to 0.3038, while ROC-AUC remained at
0.9510. On Pima Diabetes, the difference (0.7529 versus 0.7586) was within the
variation across ensembles. On the imbalanced Stroke Prediction
dataset, TabR and FiTabR did not predict any positive case at the default
decision threshold, because hyperparameters were selected by validation
accuracy. FiTabR was also not the strongest model overall: ModernNCA achieved
the highest accuracy on Heart Disease (0.9058). A component-level ablation
study showed that the components did not contribute equally across datasets.
The residual term received the clearest empirical support, since
removing it reduced accuracy on Heart Disease to 0.8647, whereas fixed
attention scaling and learned nonuniform feature weighting showed more
dataset-dependent effects. These results indicate that explicit feature-aware
numerical representation can influence retrieval-augmented tabular learning,
but that its effectiveness depends on the characteristics of the underlying
dataset.

Based on this design and experimental analysis, the principal contributions of
this thesis are summarized as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Feature-aware extension of retrieval-augmented tabular
    learning.}
    FiTabR introduces sample-specific residual feature weighting of numerical features
    before the TabR encoder while retaining the original candidate retrieval,
    contextual aggregation, and prediction mechanisms.

    \item \textbf{Cross-dataset empirical evaluation of the proposed
    representation mechanism.}
    FiTabR was compared with TabR, ModernNCA, gradient-boosted decision trees,
    and deep tabular baselines on three medical datasets, which identified
    conditions in which feature-aware representation helped and conditions in
    which it did not.

    \item \textbf{Component-level analysis through architectural ablation.}
    The residual term, attention scaling, and learned nonuniform weighting
    were evaluated separately, and the residual term received the strongest
    empirical support on Heart Disease.

    \item \textbf{Analysis of sample-specific feature emphasis.}
    The learned attention distributions were summarized by their mean and
    standard deviation across samples and compared with established medical
    knowledge.
\end{enumerate}

The content of this thesis is organized as follows. Chapter II reviews
tree-based tabular learning, deep learning methods for structured data,
retrieval-augmented tabular learning, the TabR architecture, and feature
attention approaches relevant to this work. Chapter III presents the proposed
FiTabR architecture, including the problem formulation, feature-attention
module, residual feature weighting, and integration with the TabR retrieval
framework. Chapter IV describes the experimental setup, including the
datasets, preprocessing, baseline models, evaluation metrics, and
implementation details. Chapter V presents the main comparative results, the
ablation experiments, the attention analysis, and a discussion of the observed
behavior. Finally, Chapter VI summarizes the main findings, discusses the
limitations of the study, and outlines directions for future research.

\chapter{Research Background}

\section{Classical Machine Learning for Tabular Data}

Classical machine learning methods remain strong baselines for structured
tabular prediction. In particular, gradient-boosted decision tree (GBDT)
methods are widely used because they can model nonlinear relationships and
high-order feature interactions while remaining effective on small- and
medium-sized tabular datasets. XGBoost introduced a scalable gradient-boosting
framework with regularization and efficient tree construction
\cite{chen2016xgboost}, while LightGBM improved computational efficiency
through histogram-based learning and leaf-wise tree growth
\cite{ke2017lightgbm}. CatBoost further addressed the treatment of categorical
variables through ordered boosting and specialized categorical-feature
processing \cite{prokhorenkova2018catboost}.

The continued strength of these approaches is particularly relevant to deep
learning research on tabular data. Unlike image and language domains, where
deep neural architectures have become dominant, tree-based methods frequently
remain highly competitive on conventional tabular benchmarks
\cite{grinsztajn2022tree}. This observation makes GBDT models important
reference baselines when evaluating new neural architectures for medical
tabular prediction.

Local learning provides another relevant perspective. Instead of learning only
a global mapping from features to outcomes, local methods make predictions
using observations in the neighborhood of a target sample. Early work on local
learning demonstrated the general principle of adapting prediction to nearby
training observations \cite{bottou1992local}. Similarly, standard statistical
learning methods such as $k$-nearest neighbors provide a simple example of
instance-based prediction in which similarity between observations directly
affects the predicted output \cite{james2013islr}. This local-learning
perspective is conceptually related to modern retrieval-augmented models, which
combine learned representations with information obtained from selected
training samples.


\section{Deep Learning for Tabular Data}

Recent research has investigated whether neural networks can achieve stronger
performance on structured data through architectures specifically designed for
tabular inputs. Gorishniy et al. revisited commonly used deep learning models
for tabular prediction and demonstrated that carefully configured multilayer perceptrons and
Transformer-based architectures, in particular FT-Transformer, which represents
each feature as a token and applies self-attention across features, can serve
as competitive neural baselines \cite{gorishniy2021revisiting}. Subsequent work also emphasized
the importance of numerical feature representation, showing that the manner in
which continuous variables are embedded can substantially affect the
performance of tabular neural networks \cite{gorishniy2022embeddings}.

Attention mechanisms have been applied to tabular learning in several forms.
TabTransformer uses contextual representations of categorical variables to
model relationships among features \cite{huang2020tabtransformer}. TabNet
introduced sequential attentive feature selection, allowing the model to focus
on subsets of variables at different decision steps
\cite{arik2021tabnet}. SAINT extended attention beyond feature interactions by
applying attention across both rows and columns and combining this mechanism
with contrastive pre-training \cite{somepalli2021saint}. These methods
demonstrated that attention can be used to adapt representations to the
structure of tabular data rather than treating the input only through a
standard feed-forward transformation.

Despite these developments, deep learning does not consistently dominate
tree-based methods on tabular datasets. Grinsztajn et al. showed that
tree-based models retain important advantages on many medium-sized structured
datasets, particularly under conditions commonly encountered in practical
tabular learning \cite{grinsztajn2022tree}. Consequently, recent research
explored mechanisms beyond conventional feed-forward representation learning,
including improved numerical embeddings, attention-based feature interaction,
and retrieval of information from other observations.


\section{Retrieval-Augmented Tabular Learning}
\label{sec:retrieval-augmented}
Retrieval-augmented learning extends conventional predictive models by allowing
information from other training observations to participate directly in the
prediction of a target sample. The underlying idea is related to local and
instance-based learning, but modern retrieval-augmented methods combine this
principle with learned neural representations. Instead of relying exclusively
on predefined distances in the original input space, these methods learn a
representation in which relevant observations can be identified and their
information subsequently incorporated into prediction.

Several approaches investigated interactions among observations for
tabular prediction. The Retrieval and Interaction Machine (RIM) combines
retrieval with interaction mechanisms to exploit information from related
training instances \cite{qin2021rim}. Other work investigated
neighborhood-based representation propagation as a means of enriching the
representation of tabular observations using information from nearby samples
\cite{du2022neighborhood}. Self-attention between datapoints was also
proposed as an alternative to architectures that process each observation
independently, demonstrating that relationships among samples can provide
additional predictive information \cite{kossen2021selfattention}. Related
memory-based approaches, including Hopular, further demonstrated the potential
of associating tabular observations with information stored in the training
data \cite{schafl2022hopular,ramsauer2021hopfield}.

TabR provides a more direct and computationally efficient realization of
retrieval-augmented tabular deep learning. The model combines a feed-forward
network with a nearest-neighbor-like retrieval component operating in a learned
representation space \cite{gorishniy2024tabr}. For each target observation,
TabR constructs a query representation, identifies relevant candidate
observations from the training set, and incorporates their representations and
labels through an attention-like contextual aggregation mechanism. The
resulting contextual information is combined with the target representation
before final prediction.

The TabR study demonstrated that retrieval can substantially strengthen tabular
deep learning on a range of benchmarks while using a comparatively simple
architecture \cite{gorishniy2024tabr}. Importantly, however, the effectiveness
of retrieval was shown to depend on the dataset. The study also identified
numerical representation and retrieval as distinct but interacting components
of tabular deep learning, suggesting that the representation on which
similarity is computed is itself an important architectural consideration.

ModernNCA revisited Neighbourhood Components Analysis (NCA) as a deep
baseline for tabular data \cite{ye2025modernnca}. The method learned a
nonlinear mapping of the input features into an embedding space and predicted
the label of a target observation as a weighted average of the labels of
training observations, with weights given by a softmax over negative Euclidean
distances in that space. During training, each mini-batch used only a random
subset of the training set as candidate neighbors, which reduced computation
and added stochasticity; at inference, the full training set was used. Unlike
TabR, ModernNCA did not use an attention-like context module and predicted
directly from the weighted neighbor labels. On a benchmark of 300 datasets, it
obtained a better average rank than TabR while requiring approximately half of
its training time and memory \cite{ye2025modernnca}. ModernNCA therefore
improved retrieval-based prediction by learning the representation in which
neighbors are compared, rather than by making the retrieval step more complex.

FiTabR builds on the same observation but changes the representation in a
different way. Whereas ModernNCA learns a complete embedding for the neighbor
search, FiTabR keeps the TabR encoder, retrieval and changes
the numerical representation supplied to the TabR encoder. A lightweight
attention network generates a sample-specific distribution over numerical
variables, and these values are used for residual feature weighting before encoding. FiTabR therefore provides a
controlled setting for studying whether explicit sample-specific numerical
modulation before representation learning can affect the behavior of a
retrieval-augmented tabular model.


\section{Attention and Explainability in Medical Machine Learning}

Interpretability is an important consideration in medical machine learning
because predictive performance alone may be insufficient for understanding how
a model arrives at its outputs. General-purpose post-hoc explanation methods
have therefore been widely applied to complex predictive models. LIME explains
individual predictions by constructing locally interpretable approximations of
a model around a particular observation \cite{ribeiro2016lime}, whereas SHAP
uses concepts from cooperative game theory to assign feature-attribution values
to model predictions \cite{lundberg2017shap}. More broadly, the application of
machine learning in medicine has emphasized the importance of considering model
interpretation, validation, and clinical context alongside predictive
performance \cite{rajkomar2019mlmedicine}.

Attention-based tabular models provide another source of information about
internal model behavior. TabNet, for example, exposes feature-selection masks
that indicate which variables are emphasized during different decision steps
\cite{arik2021tabnet}. In similarity-based learning, interpretable prediction
has also been studied through mechanisms that relate predictions to nearby
training observations. Wang and Sabuncu showed that a flexible
Nadaraya--Watson prediction head can provide similarity-based explanations
while supporting calibrated classification
\cite{wang2023nadarayawatson}.

FiTabR combines these two perspectives at the architectural level. TabR already
provides an instance-retrieval mechanism through which selected training
observations contribute contextual information to prediction. FiTabR
additionally produces sample-specific attention values over the numerical
features before encoding. These values create an explicit feature-level signal
that can be examined across observations.

However, attention values should not automatically be interpreted as causal
feature importance or clinical risk estimates. In this thesis, they are treated
as indicators of the internal allocation of emphasis within the FiTabR
representation mechanism. Mean attention values are used to characterize the
average emphasis assigned to individual numerical variables, while their
standard deviations are used to examine how that emphasis varies across
samples. The results were compared with established
medical knowledge as a behavioral analysis of the model.

\section{Recent Tabular Methods and Baseline Selection}
\label{sec:recent-methods}
Recent tabular learning methods were considered as additional baselines:
TabM, ModernNCA, TabPFN, TabICL, and TabICLv2. They were assessed against
three criteria derived from the research question of this thesis. First, a
baseline had to be trained and tuned on each dataset under the same protocol
as FiTabR, so that differences in results reflect the models rather than the
training regime. Second, it had to be directly comparable with a
retrieval-augmented architecture whose modification concerns the
representation used for retrieval. Third, a public implementation had to be
available.

TabM is a multilayer perceptron that imitates an ensemble of multilayer
perceptrons whose members share most of their weights, and its authors
reported it as the strongest deep tabular model in their benchmark
\cite{gorishniy2025tabm}. TabM does not retrieve training observations. Its
improvement comes from parameter-efficient ensembling, a technique that could
also be applied to other architectures, including retrieval-based ones. A
comparison with TabM would therefore mainly measure the effect of ensembling
rather than the effect of modifying the representation before retrieval, and
TabM was not included.

TabPFN \cite{hollmann2025tabpfn}, TabICL \cite{qu2025tabicl}, and TabICLv2
\cite{qu2026tabiclv2} are tabular foundation models. They were pretrained on
large collections of synthetic datasets and make predictions for a new dataset
in a single forward pass, using its training observations as context without
updating their parameters. TabPFN was designed for datasets with up to 10,000
samples, a range that includes the three datasets of this thesis, whereas
TabICL and TabICLv2 were designed to scale to hundreds of thousands and
millions of samples. Because these models are not trained on the target
dataset, they cannot be tuned under the protocol described in
Section~\ref{sec:implementation-details}, and the proposed feature-attention
module cannot be inserted into them. A comparison would contrast large-scale
pretraining with per-dataset training rather than address the design question
of this thesis. They were therefore not used as baselines, and a comparison
with foundation models is left for future work
(Section~\ref{sec:future-work}).

ModernNCA \cite{ye2025modernnca}, described in
Section~\ref{sec:retrieval-augmented}, met all three criteria and was
selected. It is trained and tuned on each dataset, predicts from the labels of
retrieved training observations, and, like FiTabR, obtains its improvement by
changing the representation in which neighbors are compared. It was also
proposed as a simpler alternative to TabR. ModernNCA therefore provides the
most direct test of whether the feature reweighting of FiTabR adds value
beyond the most recent retrieval-based method.
\section{Research Gap and Positioning of FiTabR}

Existing work has therefore addressed several complementary aspects of tabular
learning. Gradient-boosted decision trees provide strong non-neural baselines
\cite{chen2016xgboost,ke2017lightgbm,prokhorenkova2018catboost};
attention-based neural architectures improve feature representation and
interaction modeling
\cite{huang2020tabtransformer,arik2021tabnet,somepalli2021saint}; and
retrieval-augmented approaches incorporate information from other training
observations
\cite{qin2021rim,kossen2021selfattention,gorishniy2024tabr,ye2025modernnca}.

Within the methods considered in this thesis, however, the effect of explicitly
introducing sample-specific modulation of numerical variables before the TabR
encoder and retrieval representation remains insufficiently characterized.
TabR learns the representation on which candidate similarity is evaluated, but
does not explicitly introduce a separate sample-specific numerical
feature-modulation stage before encoding. Conversely, attention-based tabular
models provide mechanisms for feature interaction or selection, but are not
designed as controlled modifications of the TabR retrieval framework.

FiTabR addresses this gap by introducing residual feature weighting of numerical inputs
before the TabR encoder. This design
isolates the representation-level modification from changes to the retrieval
architecture and enables three related questions to be studied: whether the
modification improves predictive behavior, which components of the
feature-modulation mechanism contribute to any observed differences, and how
the learned feature emphasis behaves across medical datasets.

The contribution is therefore not based on the assumption that feature-aware
modulation is universally superior to the original TabR representation.
Instead, FiTabR was evaluated as a controlled architectural extension whose
effectiveness may depend on the characteristics of the dataset. Ablation
experiments and attention analysis were used to determine the
conditions under which the proposed representation mechanism provides
measurable value and to identify its limitations.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

This study proposes FiTabR, a feature-attention extension of TabR for binary
classification on medical tabular datasets. The central idea is to modify the
numerical input representation before it enters the TabR encoder, so that the
model can assign sample-specific emphasis to numerical features before
similarities between samples are computed.

Let the dataset be defined as $\mathcal{D}=\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$, where
$x_i$ represents the feature vector of sample $i$, $y_i\in\{0,1\}$ is its
binary class label, and $N$ is the number of samples. The label indicates the
presence or absence of the target clinical outcome. Each input consists of
numerical and categorical features,
$x_i=(x_i^{\mathrm{num}},x_i^{\mathrm{cat}})$, where
$x_i^{\mathrm{num}}\in\mathbb{R}^{F}$ contains the $F$ numerical features and
$x_i^{\mathrm{cat}}$ contains the categorical features. The objective is to
learn a prediction function $f(x_i)\rightarrow\hat{y}_i$, where $\hat{y}_i$
denotes the predicted probability that sample $i$ belongs to the positive
class.

Medical tabular datasets contain heterogeneous variables whose predictive
contributions may differ across observations. A feature that is informative
for one patient may therefore be less informative for another. In a
retrieval-based model, this matters for more than the final prediction,
because the input representation also determines which training patients are
treated as similar to the target patient.

TabR does not adapt to this variation. Every sample passes through the same
encoder, so a numerical feature that the encoder emphasizes for one patient is
emphasized in the same way for every other patient. In the configuration used
in this thesis, the encoder was a single linear layer without numerical
embeddings, and this treatment was therefore one fixed linear weighting shared
by all samples. Weakly related or noisy features then enter every distance
computation with the same weight and can move a patient closer to clinically
different neighbors. FiTabR replaces this fixed treatment of the numerical
input with a sample-dependent feature weighting.

TabR and ModernNCA, the two retrieval-based models described in
Section~\ref{sec:retrieval-augmented}, were both candidates for the base
architecture. TabR was selected because its contextual value is built from the
difference between the target and neighbor keys
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:contextual_value}). A change in the input representation
therefore affects TabR in two ways: it changes which neighbors are retrieved,
and it changes the information that each neighbor passes to the prediction.
ModernNCA predicts directly from the distance-weighted labels of the retrieved
neighbors~\cite{ye2025modernnca}, so the same change would affect only the
neighbor weights. ModernNCA was therefore included as a separate
retrieval-based baseline.

\section{Proposed FiTabR Architecture}
\label{sec:proposed}

FiTabR introduces a feature-attention module before the TabR encoder (Figure~\ref{fig:fitabr_architecture}). The
module learns a sample-specific distribution over numerical features and uses
it to modify the original numerical measurements. For each observation, the
processing sequence is $x_i^{\mathrm{num}} \rightarrow a_i \rightarrow
\tilde{x}_i^{\mathrm{num}} \rightarrow \tilde{x}_i \rightarrow z_i \rightarrow
k_i \rightarrow \hat{y}_i$, where $a_i$ denotes the attention vector,
$\tilde{x}_i^{\mathrm{num}}$ the weighted numerical representation,
$\tilde{x}_i$ the fused input, $z_i$ the encoded representation, and $k_i$ the
retrieval key.

\centering

\caption[Architecture of the proposed FiTabR model.]{Architecture of FiTabR. A
feature-attention module generates sample-specific weights for numerical

\label{fig:fitabr_architecture}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Feature-Attention Module}
\label{sec:attention}

The feature-attention module estimates the relative emphasis assigned to each
numerical feature using a two-layer neural transformation:
\begin{equation}
a_i =
\operatorname{softmax}
\left(
W_2 \operatorname{ReLU} \left(W_1 x_i^{\mathrm{num}} + b_1\right) + b_2
\right),
\label{eq:feature_attention}
\end{equation}
where $W_1,W_2\in\mathbb{R}^{F\times F}$ are trainable weight matrices,
$b_1,b_2\in\mathbb{R}^{F}$ are bias terms, and $\operatorname{ReLU}(\cdot)$
denotes the rectified linear unit activation function. The ReLU activation
allows the module to learn nonlinear relationships between numerical variables
when estimating their relative emphasis. Because the module receives the whole
numerical vector, the weight of one feature can depend on the values of the
others. The module adds $2F^2+2F$ parameters: 84 for Heart Disease ($F=6$),
144 for Pima Diabetes ($F=8$), and 60 for Stroke Prediction ($F=5$). This is
less than 0.1\% of the parameters of the selected FiTabR models. The module is
trained jointly with the rest of the network and has no separate objective.

Softmax is applied across the feature dimension because it produces a
normalized distribution of attention weights, with $a_{i,j}>0$ and
$\sum_{j=1}^{F}a_{i,j}=1$. Each feature therefore competes with the other
numerical features for a share of the total attention. A larger weight on one
feature implies a smaller weight on the others, and every sample has the same
total emphasis to distribute. Independent gates, such as a sigmoid per
feature, could raise all features together, which would amount to a global
rescaling rather than a choice among features. The normalization also gives a
consistent scale for comparing feature emphasis across observations, which the
analysis in Section~\ref{sec:attention_stats} relies on.

\subsection{Attention Scaling and Residual Feature Weighting}
\label{sec:residual}

After computing the attention vector, FiTabR scales it as $a_i' = \alpha a_i$,
where $\alpha$ is the attention scaling coefficient. The scaled attention
vector is then applied to the original numerical input through residual
feature weighting:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{x}_i^{\mathrm{num}}
=
x_i^{\mathrm{num}}
\odot
\left(1 + a_i'\right),
\label{eq:residual_feature_weighting}
\end{equation}
where $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication. Feature $j$ of sample $i$
is thus multiplied by $1+a'_{i,j}=1+\alpha a_{i,j}$.

The residual formulation is important because applying the attention directly,
as $x_i^{\mathrm{num}}\odot a_i'$, would make each feature depend only on its
normalized attention weight. Since the weights sum to one, the average
multiplier would be $\alpha/F$, which is below one for all three datasets.
Most features would then be shrunk toward zero, and a feature receiving a
small weight would almost disappear before the encoder processes it. On small
clinical datasets, this risks discarding a measurement on the basis of a
poorly estimated attention value. By adding the attention weights to one, the
residual formulation keeps every multiplier between $1$ and $1+\alpha$. Every
numerical feature therefore retains its original contribution, while features
receiving greater attention are amplified more strongly.

The coefficient $\alpha$ controls the magnitude of this additional modulation.
Because the attention weights sum to one, $\alpha$ is the total extra emphasis
distributed over the $F$ features, and the average multiplier is $1+\alpha/F$.
In this thesis, $\alpha$ was fixed at 2.0 as an implementation hyperparameter
rather than a theoretically derived constant, and it was not tuned. A single
feature could therefore be at most tripled, and the average multiplier was
1.33 for Heart Disease, 1.25 for Pima Diabetes, and 1.40 for Stroke
Prediction. The No Scale ablation in Section~\ref{sec:ablation} removes the
coefficient while retaining the residual feature weighting, and therefore tests whether the
fixed value provides a measurable benefit.

The numerical features were quantile-transformed to an approximately normal
distribution centered at the training median
(Section~\ref{sec:preprocessing}). A multiplier above one therefore stretches
the deviation of a feature from its typical value, so that differences in that
feature between two patients count more in the subsequent distance
computation.

\subsection{Feature Fusion}
\label{sec:fusion}

After residual feature weighting, the numerical representation is combined
with the categorical features. Categorical variables are represented using
one-hot encoding, producing the categorical representation
$e_i^{\mathrm{cat}}$, and the fused input is
$\tilde{x}_i=\operatorname{Concat}(\tilde{x}_i^{\mathrm{num}},
e_i^{\mathrm{cat}})$. The attention mechanism therefore operates only on
numerical variables, and the categorical features are not reweighted.

\section{Integration with TabR Retrieval}
\label{sec:integration}

The fused representation is processed by the TabR encoder,
$z_i=E(\tilde{x}_i)$, and projected into the retrieval key space as $k_i=W_K
z_i$, where $W_K$ is a learnable key projection matrix. For a target
observation $q$ and a candidate observation $i$, the retrieval similarity is
the negative squared Euclidean distance between their keys:
\begin{equation}
S(z_q, z_i)
=
-
\left\|k_q - k_i\right\|_2^2.
\label{eq:retrieval_similarity}
\end{equation}
The $m$ candidates with the highest similarity are selected as the context of
the target observation. In all experiments, the context size was $m=96$.

The placement of feature attention before the encoder is central to the
proposed design. The candidate keys are computed by the same encoder as the
target key. Feature weighting therefore changes the representation of both the
target and every candidate, and it can change which training observations are
retrieved. A module placed after retrieval could only change how a fixed set
of neighbors is used.

Because the encoder was a single linear layer in the configuration used here
(Section~\ref{sec:problem}) and the key projection is also linear, the key
difference $k_q-k_i$ depends on the input difference $\tilde{x}_q-\tilde{x}_i$
through one fixed matrix that is the same for every pair of observations. In
TabR, this matrix acts directly on $x_q-x_i$, so all pairs are compared with
the same weighting of the input features. In FiTabR, the numerical features of
each observation are first multiplied by its own factors $1+a_i'$, so the
comparison is no longer identical for every pair. This is the specific change
that FiTabR introduces into retrieval.

For each retrieved candidate, TabR constructs a contextual value from the
candidate label and the difference between the two keys:
\begin{equation}
V(z_q, z_i, y_i)
=
W_Y(y_i) + T(k_q - k_i),
\label{eq:contextual_value}
\end{equation}
where $W_Y(y_i)$ denotes the label embedding and $T(\cdot)$ is a learnable
transformation of the key difference. Because the value depends on $k_q-k_i$,
the feature weighting also changes the information that each retrieved
neighbor contributes. The values are averaged with softmax weights computed
from the similarities to produce the contextual representation $C_q$. The
prediction head then produces the final output $\hat{y}_q = P(z_q, C_q)$,
where $C_q$ is added to the target representation $z_q$ before the head, so
that the target representation reaches the predictor alongside the retrieved
information.

TabR and FiTabR were tuned independently with the same search space and
validation procedure (Section~\ref{sec:implementation-details}). Their
architectures differ only in the module described in
Section~\ref{sec:proposed}, but their selected hyperparameters may differ. The
comparison between them is therefore architecturally controlled, and it
reflects both the module and its effect on the selected configuration.

\section{Attention-Based Feature Analysis}
\label{sec:attention_stats}

FiTabR produces an attention vector for each observation, which gives a
sample-specific measure of the relative emphasis assigned to numerical
features before encoding. To summarize these vectors across the dataset,
FiTabR computes the mean attention score $\mu_a = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
a_i$ and the standard deviation $\sigma_a =
\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(a_i - \mu_a)^2}$, where the square is taken
element-wise and $N$ here denotes the number of training observations. The
reported statistics were calculated from the FiTabR model trained with seed~0
on each dataset. The mean indicates the average relative attention assigned to
each feature, and the standard deviation describes how much that assignment
varies across training observations. Under uniform attention every mean equals
$1/F$, which serves as the reference value in the attention analysis of
Chapter~\ref{chap:results}. These statistics describe the internal
feature-weighting behavior of FiTabR, not clinical or causal importance;
Section~\ref{sec:clinical_attention} discusses how they are interpreted.

FiTabR was evaluated on three medical binary-classification datasets. This chapter describes

\section{Preprocessing}
\label{sec:preprocessing}

A common preprocessing pipeline was applied to all models. Each preprocessing operation was fitted using only the training partition and was subsequently applied to the validation and test partitions without refitting. This procedure prevented validation or test information from influencing model training.

Each dataset was divided into disjoint training, validation, and test subsets using an approximate ratio of 70:15:15. Stratified partitioning was used to preserve the class distribution across the subsets. The training subset was used to estimate model parameters, the validation subset was used for hyperparameter selection and early stopping, and the test subset was reserved for final evaluation.

Numerical features were processed using quantile transformation. This operation reduced the effect of extreme values and differences in feature scale. The transformation was estimated from the training subset and then applied to the validation and test subsets. Categorical variables were integer encoded using mappings derived from the training data. The same mappings were retained for the remaining subsets.

Missing numerical values were imputed with the median of the corresponding feature, calculated from the training subset. Unmeasured values are recorded as zero in the original data (for example, cholesterol, insulin, and skin thickness); these zero values were kept as recorded and not treated as missing. Missing categorical values were represented by a dedicated category or processed according to the categorical policy supported by the corresponding model. Validation- and test-set statistics were not used during imputation.

Synthetic oversampling was not applied in the principal experiments. The original class distributions were preserved to avoid introducing artificial observations into the retrieval candidate memory. The effects of class imbalance were assessed using recall, F1-score, and receiver operating characteristic area under the curve (ROC-AUC), in addition to accuracy.

For the retrieval-based models, the candidate memory contained only training observations. Validation and test observations were not inserted into the memory. During training, the current target observation was excluded from its own candidate context. These restrictions prevented self-retrieval and label leakage.

\section{Baselines}
\label{sec:baselines}

FiTabR was compared with gradient-boosted decision trees, conventional neural
networks, attention-based deep tabular models, and two retrieval-based models,
TabR and ModernNCA.

\subsection{Gradient-Boosted Decision Trees}

XGBoost~\cite{chen2016xgboost}, LightGBM~\cite{ke2017lightgbm}, and CatBoost~\cite{prokhorenkova2018catboost} were used as tree-based baselines, as they provide strong non-neural reference points for small and medium-sized tabular datasets. CatBoost was also included because Heart Disease and Stroke Prediction contain categorical variables.

\subsection{Deep Tabular Models}

A multilayer perceptron (MLP)~\cite{rumelhart1986learning} served as the basic neural baseline; it processes each observation independently, without feature attention or retrieval. FT-Transformer~\cite{gorishniy2021revisiting} represents each feature as a token and applies self-attention to model interactions among features, and SAINT~\cite{somepalli2021saint} provides an additional attention-based comparison.

\subsection{Retrieval-Based Baselines}

TabR~\cite{gorishniy2024tabr} was used as the principal baseline. FiTabR differs from it only in the representation module described in Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}, so the comparison shows whether this module changes classification performance.

ModernNCA~\cite{ye2025modernnca} was used as a second retrieval-based baseline. It learns an embedding of the input features and predicts from the distance-weighted labels of training observations, without the context module of TabR. It therefore provides a recent retrieval-based reference that improves the representation used for the neighbor search rather than the retrieval step itself; the reasons for its selection are given in Section~\ref{sec:recent-methods}.


\section{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

Performance was evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, ROC-AUC, and binary cross-entropy. The first four metrics were computed from the classification decisions, whereas ROC-AUC and binary cross-entropy were computed from predicted probabilities.

Accuracy measures the proportion of correctly classified observations:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Accuracy}=
\frac{TP+TN}{TP+TN+FP+FN},
\label{eq:accuracy}
\end{equation}
where $TP$, $TN$, $FP$, and $FN$ denote true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives, respectively.

Precision measures the proportion of positive predictions that are correct:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}.
\label{eq:precision}
\end{equation}

Recall measures the proportion of positive observations correctly identified:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Recall}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}.
\label{eq:recall}
\end{equation}
Recall is particularly relevant to medical classification because false-negative predictions may cause affected patients to be missed. Recall is reported for the positive class, whereas precision and F1-score are reported as weighted averages over the two classes.


The F1-score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{F1}=2\frac{\mathrm{Precision}\times\mathrm{Recall}}
{\mathrm{Precision}+\mathrm{Recall}}.
\label{eq:f1}
\end{equation}
It provides a balanced measure when both false-positive and false-negative errors are relevant.



ROC-AUC measures the ability of a model to rank positive observations above negative observations across classification thresholds. A value of 0.5 indicates random ranking, whereas a value of 1.0 indicates perfect class discrimination. This metric was included because it is less dependent on a single decision threshold than accuracy or F1-score.


Binary cross-entropy evaluates the quality of the predicted probabilities:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\left[y_i\log(\hat{p}_i)+(1-y_i)\log(1-\hat{p}_i)\right],
\label{eq:bce}
\end{equation}
where $y_i$ is the true label and $\hat{p}_i$ is the predicted probability of the positive class. Lower values indicate better probabilistic predictions.

For the imbalanced Stroke Prediction dataset, ROC-AUC, cross-entropy, and recall were considered together with accuracy. This avoided drawing conclusions from majority-class performance alone.


\section{Implementation Details}
\label{sec:implementation-details}

The models were implemented in Python within a shared experimental framework, with dataset processing and metric computation based on scikit-learn~\cite{sklearn}. Hyperparameters were optimized with Optuna using the Tree-structured Parzen Estimator sampler~\cite{optuna}. The repository configurations used 100 optimization trials, and hyperparameters were selected exclusively on validation performance.

For the neural models, the search space included the learning rate, weight decay, hidden representation dimension, and dropout, as well as the number of blocks for the MLP, FT-Transformer, SAINT, and ModernNCA; the batch size was fixed at 256. The attention-based models additionally included attention dropout and attention-specific representation parameters. FiTabR used the same search space as TabR; the feature-attention module added no tuned hyperparameters, and $\alpha$ was fixed at 2.0. For ModernNCA, the search space further included the fraction of training observations sampled as candidate neighbors in each mini-batch, and the AdamW optimizer was used instead of the stochastic gradient descent of the original study.

For the tree-based baselines, the search space included the learning rate, tree depth, number of boosting iterations, regularization coefficients, bagging parameters, and leaf-estimation settings. Early stopping was applied on the validation subset.

All neural models were trained by mini-batch gradient-based optimization with binary cross-entropy as the objective. For the retrieval-based models, the candidate memory consisted of the complete training partition.

Each selected configuration was trained with 15 random seeds (0--14), which were divided into three groups:
\begin{equation}
\{0,1,2,3,4\},\quad
\{5,6,7,8,9\},\quad
\{10,11,12,13,14\}.
\label{eq:seed-groups}
\end{equation}
Predicted probabilities were averaged within each group to form three five-model ensembles. For a metric $m$, the mean over the $R=3$ ensembles was calculated as
\begin{equation}
\bar{m}=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}m_r,
\label{eq:metric-mean}
\end{equation}
and the sample standard deviation as
\begin{equation}
s_m=\sqrt{\frac{1}{R-1}\sum_{r=1}^{R}(m_r-\bar{m})^2}.
\label{eq:metric-std}
\end{equation}

The direct comparison between FiTabR and TabR used identical preprocessing, data partitions, candidate memory, retrieval settings, optimization objective, early-stopping procedure, random seeds, and evaluation metrics. 
All experiments were conducted using Python 3.9.16 and PyTorch 1.13.1 on a system equipped with an Intel Core i7-7700K CPU operating at 4.20 GHz, 32 GB of RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce GTX 1050 GPU with 2 GB of dedicated memory. The software environment included CUDA 11.7.99, scikit-learn 1.2.2, XGBoost 1.7.4, LightGBM 3.3.5, CatBoost 1.1.1, and Optuna 3.1.0.

\section{Heart Disease}
\label{sec:heart_results}

On the Heart Disease dataset (Table~\ref{tab:heart_results}), ModernNCA~\cite{ye2025modernnca}
achieved the highest mean accuracy (0.9058), precision, F1-score, and ROC-AUC
(0.9527). FiTabR ranked second on the threshold-dependent metrics, with an
accuracy of 0.8937 and an F1-score of 0.8940. It exceeded TabR, the
gradient-boosted models (CatBoost, XGBoost, and LightGBM), and the deep tabular
baselines (MLP, SAINT, and FT-Transformer) on accuracy, precision, and
F1-score. CatBoost was the closest of these baselines, with an F1-score of
0.8890. Its gap to FiTabR is about one standard deviation and corresponds to
fewer than one patient on the 138-sample test set.

Relative to its direct TabR baseline, FiTabR increased accuracy from 0.8841 to
0.8937, an absolute gain of 0.96 percentage points. Precision and F1-score
changed in the same direction. The two models obtained the same mean ROC-AUC
(0.9510), so the feature-attention module did not improve how TabR ranks
positive and negative patients. The clearer change appeared in cross-entropy,
which decreased from 0.3586 to 0.3038, a difference larger than either standard
deviation. This was the lowest mean cross-entropy in the comparison, although
ModernNCA was practically identical (0.3039). Among the non-retrieval models,
LightGBM obtained both the strongest ROC-AUC (0.9428) and the lowest
cross-entropy (0.3281). An unchanged ROC-AUC together with a lower

Across the three datasets, the effect of FiTabR relative to TabR was
dataset-dependent. On Heart Disease, FiTabR increased accuracy and
substantially reduced cross-entropy, while ROC-AUC remained unchanged. On Pima

across ensembles, and ROC-AUC did not improve. On Stroke Prediction, TabR and

probability-based metric changed beyond its standard deviation. FiTabR did not
achieve the best accuracy or ROC-AUC on any dataset: ModernNCA led on Heart
Disease, FT-Transformer on Pima Diabetes, and LightGBM and FT-Transformer on
Stroke Prediction. Section~\ref{sec:dataset_discussion} relates these
differences to the characteristics of each dataset after the ablation and
attention results have been presented.

but they do not identify which elements of the feature-aware representation
are responsible for the observed changes. The following ablation study
therefore isolates the residual term, fixed attention scaling, and
learned nonuniform weighting. A subsequent feature-attention analysis then
examines the model-internal emphasis assigned to the numerical variables,
without treating the attention values as causal or clinical importance
scores.

\section{Ablation Study}
\label{sec:ablation}

Recall that FiTabR modifies the numerical representation before the
TabR encoder and retrieval stage. As described in the Methodology
section, for the $i$-th sample, the feature-attention module produces
the sample-specific attention vector $a_i$ from the numerical input
$x_i^{\mathrm{num}}$. The attention vector is scaled as

\begin{equation}
a_i' = \alpha a_i,
\qquad \alpha = 2.0,
\label{eq:ablation_scale}
\end{equation}

and the complete FiTabR model applies residual feature weighting as

\begin{equation}
\tilde{x}_i^{\mathrm{num}}
=
x_i^{\mathrm{num}}
\odot
\left(1+a_i'\right),
\label{eq:ablation_fitabr}
\end{equation}

where $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication.

To determine which components of this representation mechanism are
important, three ablated configurations were evaluated. The first removes
the residual term, the second removes the fixed attention scaling
coefficient, and the third replaces the learned sample-specific
attention vector with uniform feature weights. TabR was included as the
reference configuration. The resulting numerical representations are

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\text{TabR:}\quad
&
\tilde{x}_{i}^{\mathrm{num}}
=
x_i^{\mathrm{num}},
\\[3pt]
\text{FiTabR:}\quad
&
\tilde{x}_{i}^{\mathrm{num}}
=
x_i^{\mathrm{num}}
\odot
\left(1+\alpha a_i\right),
\\[3pt]
\text{No Residual:}\quad
&
\tilde{x}_{i,\mathrm{NR}}^{\mathrm{num}}
=
x_i^{\mathrm{num}}
\odot
\left(\alpha a_i\right),
\\[3pt]
\text{No Scale:}\quad
&
\tilde{x}_{i,\mathrm{NS}}^{\mathrm{num}}
=
x_i^{\mathrm{num}}
\odot
\left(1+a_i\right),
\\[3pt]
\text{Uniform:}\quad
&
\tilde{x}_{i,\mathrm{U}}^{\mathrm{num}}
=
x_i^{\mathrm{num}}
\odot
\left(1+\alpha a_i^{\mathrm{U}}\right),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:ablation_variants}
\end{equation}

where

\begin{equation}
a_i^{\mathrm{U}}
=
\left[
\frac{1}{F},
\ldots,
\frac{1}{F}
\right],
\label{eq:ablation_uniform}
\end{equation}

and $F$ denotes the number of numerical features. All other components
of TabR, including the encoder, candidate retrieval, contextual
aggregation, and prediction head, were kept unchanged. Therefore, the
experiment isolates the effect of the proposed feature-weighting
mechanism.

For each configuration, three independent five-model ensembles were evaluated,
covering 15 trained models in total. The reported values
represent the mean and sample standard deviation across the three
ensembles.

\subsection{Discussion}
\label{subsec:ablation_discussion}

Overall, the ablation study showed that the effect of the FiTabR
components depended on the dataset. The clearest result was
observed on Heart Disease. On this dataset, complete FiTabR improved over
TabR, whereas removing the residual term caused a substantial
performance drop. This provides evidence that preserving the original
numerical representation while applying feature-dependent modulation is
important for the proposed approach.

The results provide weaker support for the fixed scaling coefficient.
The No Scale configuration remained competitive with, and was numerically
slightly better than, complete FiTabR on Heart Disease. Since the
difference was small relative to the reported ensemble variation, the
experiment does not establish that $\alpha=2.0$ is preferable to using
the unscaled attention distribution.

The Uniform variant provides an additional control for the learned
attention mechanism. Its competitive performance on Heart Disease
indicates that numerical feature modulation may be the reason for 
the observed improvement. However, its lower ROC-AUC
on Stroke Prediction shows that uniform reweighting is not consistently
beneficial across datasets.

Pima Diabetes showed only small differences between the configurations,
while Stroke Prediction showed no improvement in threshold-based
classification performance. Therefore, the ablation results do not
support a claim that the complete FiTabR configuration is universally
superior to TabR.

Instead, the experiments indicate that explicit pre-retrieval feature
reweighting can be beneficial under some dataset conditions, with the
strongest evidence obtained on Heart Disease. Among the examined design
choices, the residual term received the clearest support on Heart Disease, 
while the contribution of fixed attention scaling and learned
sample-specific weighting was more dataset-dependent.
\section{Clinical Concordance of Learned Feature Attention}
\label{sec:clinical_attention}

signals to expose aspects of model behavior \cite{arik2021tabnet}, but
attention weights are not necessarily faithful explanations of predictions
\cite{jain2019attention}. FiTabR attention is therefore treated as
model-internal feature emphasis, not as a causal effect, clinical risk
coefficient, or measure of medical importance. The mean attention values of
the selected FiTabR models, taken from the experiment reports, were compared
qualitatively with established clinical knowledge

\subsection{Attention Analysis Summary}
\label{subsec:attention_summary}

Across the three datasets, the learned attention agreed with clinical
knowledge only in part. Cholesterol and age on Heart Disease, adiposity- and
insulin-related variables on Pima Diabetes, and average glucose level and age
on Stroke Prediction received high attention, whereas glucose on Pima
Diabetes and hypertension on Stroke Prediction received low attention despite
their central clinical roles. This partial agreement is consistent with
reading FiTabR attention as model-specific feature emphasis rather than as a
clinical ranking of risk factors.
\section{Dataset Characteristics and Differences in Effect}
\label{sec:dataset_discussion}

Table~\ref{tab:summary_results} summarizes the main results of the three
datasets. The comparison with the baselines, the ablation study, and the
attention analysis showed that FiTabR changed the behavior of TabR on Heart
Disease but not on Pima Diabetes or Stroke Prediction. Three characteristics
of the datasets help to explain this pattern: the class balance relative to
the model-selection rule, the share of the input that passes through the
attention module, and the coding of missing measurements. The size of the
test sets also limits how small a difference can be detected.

On Heart Disease, the classes were balanced (55.3\% positive), so selection
by validation accuracy did not push the models toward a single class, and
changes in the representation could appear in the threshold-dependent
metrics. All three FiTabR ensembles were more accurate than TabR, and
cross-entropy decreased from $0.3586 \pm 0.0267$ to $0.3038 \pm 0.0097$.
The absolute gain was nevertheless small: the accuracy difference of 0.0096
corresponds to about 1.3 of the 138 test patients. Only the six numerical
features pass through the attention module, while the five categorical
features reach the encoder unchanged. Among the numerical features, 18.7\%
of the patients have a recorded cholesterol value of zero, which denotes a
missing measurement rather than a clinical value. Cholesterol received the
highest and most variable attention (Section~\ref{subsec:heart_attention}),
so part of this emphasis may reflect missingness rather than cardiovascular
risk.

Pima Diabetes is the smallest dataset. With 116 test patients, one patient
corresponds to an accuracy difference of 0.0086, and the FiTabR--TabR
difference of 0.0057 was smaller than this. All eight features are
numerical, so the attention module reweights the complete input. Two of
these features contain many zero-coded values: insulin in 48.7\% of the
patients and skin thickness in 29.6\%. These two features received the
highest attention, whereas glucose, which is central to diabetes diagnosis
and is zero in fewer than 1\% of the patients, received low attention
(Section~\ref{subsec:pima_attention}). This pattern is consistent with the
module emphasizing where measurements are missing rather than metabolic
risk, although the experiment cannot separate the two. Together with the
small test set, it may explain why no FiTabR component had a consistent
effect on this dataset.

On Stroke Prediction, only 38 of the 767 test observations (4.9\%) were
positive. Because hyperparameters were selected by validation accuracy at a
threshold of 0.5, predicting every observation as negative already reached
an accuracy of 0.9505. MLP, SAINT, FT-Transformer, TabR, ModernNCA, and
FiTabR all converged to this solution, and the positive-class recall of
TabR and FiTabR was zero in every ensemble. The Stroke experiment therefore
cannot show whether FiTabR changes the detection of stroke patients. This
outcome follows from the selection protocol on a strongly imbalanced
dataset, not from the attention module itself. On the threshold-independent
metrics, FiTabR and TabR differed by less than one standard deviation in
both ROC-AUC ($0.8095 \pm 0.0126$ versus $0.8136 \pm 0.0078$) and
cross-entropy ($0.1750 \pm 0.0091$ versus $0.1780 \pm 0.0050$). In
addition, two of the five numerical features, hypertension and heart
disease, are binary indicators, which leaves less within-feature variation
for the module to reweight.

Across the three datasets, the effect of FiTabR therefore depended less on
the module alone than on the conditions under which it was evaluated: the
balance of the classes under accuracy-based selection, the part of the input
that the module reweights, and whether zero values encode missing
measurements. Because these conditions vary together across only three
datasets, this thesis cannot isolate their individual contributions. The
resulting limitations are summarized in Section~\ref{sec:future-work}.
\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{chap:conclusion}

\section{Summary of the Thesis}

This thesis investigated whether sample-specific modulation of numerical
features can improve retrieval-augmented classification on medical tabular
data. FiTabR extends TabR with a lightweight attention module that reweights
the numerical inputs before
the TabR encoder, while retrieval and prediction remain unchanged. FiTabR was
compared with TabR, ModernNCA, gradient-boosted decision trees, and deep
tabular baselines on the Heart Disease, Pima Diabetes, and Stroke Prediction
datasets.

The three research questions can be answered as follows. First, the effect of
feature modulation depended on the dataset. On Heart Disease, FiTabR improved
accuracy and probability estimates over TabR without changing ROC-AUC. On Pima
Diabetes, the differences were within the variation across ensembles, and on
Stroke Prediction the effect could not be measured under accuracy-based model
selection. FiTabR did not achieve the best accuracy or ROC-AUC on any dataset.
Second, the residual term was the component with the clearest support,
whereas the fixed scaling coefficient received no support and the benefit of
learned nonuniform weighting remained unresolved. Third, the learned attention
agreed with established medical knowledge only in part and was influenced by
zero-coded missing values, so it should be read as model-specific emphasis
rather than clinical importance.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:future-work}

The conclusions are limited by the use of three public datasets with a single
split each and small test sets, by zero-coded missing values that were not
treated as missing, and by model selection based on validation accuracy at a
fixed threshold of 0.5, which led to majority-class predictions on Stroke
Prediction. In addition, the experiments used TabR without numerical
embeddings, the scaling coefficient was fixed, categorical features were not
modulated, and the attention analysis was not verified by perturbation or
attribution tests \cite{jain2019attention,wiegreffe2019attention}.

These limitations suggest the main directions for further work. Imbalance-aware
model selection and threshold tuning would allow the effect of FiTabR on
minority-class detection to be measured. Explicit missingness indicators would
separate clinical emphasis from missingness. The module could also be combined
with the numerical embeddings of the full TabR configuration, applied to other
retrieval-based models such as ModernNCA, and examined with attribution
methods such as SHAP \cite{lundberg2017shap}. Finally, evaluation on larger,
multi-institution clinical datasets would test whether the findings
generalize beyond public benchmarks.

\section{Concluding Remarks}

Changing only the numerical representation supplied to a retrieval-augmented
model changed its behavior on one of the three datasets. Feature-aware
representation is therefore a dataset-dependent design choice for
retrieval-augmented medical tabular learning, and its benefit should be
established empirically for each prediction task.
